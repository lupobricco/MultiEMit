\section{Related Works}
\label{sec:related_works}

As affective computing shifts from sentiment polarity \cite{SOMSA} toward discrete emotion recognition, recent large scale initiatives like BRIGHTER \cite{brighter_project} highlight a persistent scarcity of native, language specific datasets. Because emotional expression is deeply contextual and shaped by cultural and linguistic norms, translated resources fail to capture fine-grained taxonomies such as Plutchik's Wheel of Emotions \cite{Plutchik}. While Italian is represented by foundational text corpora like FEEL-IT \cite{bianchi2021feel}, resolving the ambiguity of Plutchik's eight emotion schema in highly contextual domains—such as social media commentary on television broadcasts—remains a complex challenge. To address these nuances, we introduce \textit{MultiEMiT}, the first Italian multimodal dataset featuring multi-level annotations designed to capture both textual and visual emotional cues.

Early Italian resources focused strictly on binary or three class polarity. The first dedicated shift toward fine-grained categories came with \textit{FEEL-IT} \cite{bianchi2021feel}, which collected 2,037 tweets across politics, sports, and healthcare during 2020. Annotated with Ekman's four basic emotions (anger, joy, sadness, fear), FEEL-IT established that Italian language models like UmBERTo \cite{musixmatch-2020-umberto} produce reliable affective classifiers. Yet a four class schema misses mixed feelings and conversational nuances common in viewer reactions. To address broader emotional ranges, \textit{MultiEmotions-It} \cite{MultiEmotion} annotated YouTube and Facebook comments across Plutchik's eight primary emotions \cite{Plutchik}, polarity, and sarcasm. Their findings showed that while polarity models perform consistently, multi-label emotion recognition and sarcasm detection suffer from severe class sparsity and label ambiguity.

Second screen platforms have changed how broadcasters analyze viewer engagement \cite{SocialTV}. Traditional television ratings measure raw viewer counts; they cannot infer whether an audience is laughing, outraged, or sympathetic. The \textit{M.EMO.RAI} project was formed to address this limitation, establishing a joint partnership between the University of Turin and Rai (Radiotelevisione Italiana) \cite{memorai}. The goal was an automated system to track audience reactions during live broadcasts by ingesting public social network commentary.

This partnership led to the \textit{EMiT} (Emotions in Italian) corpus, benchmarked as an EVALITA 2023 shared task \cite{emit}. With 5,966 social media posts from live broadcasts (including the Sanremo Festival and scripted drama), EMiT adopted Plutchik's taxonomy. It also introduced the \textit{Target} dimension, distinguishing whether a comment attacks the broadcast \textit{Content} (the topic, guest, or political issue) or evaluates the \textit{Container} (the host, studio format, or network production). EVALITA benchmark results showed that while classifiers perform well on familiar shows, accuracy drops on out-of-domain broadcasts. To expand training coverage and alleviate class sparsity on long-tail categories, we concatenate external EMiT data to the MultiEMiT training split when fine-tuning our emotion models, while evaluating strictly on held-out MultiEMiT test instances.

Irony detection requires models to recognize when a speaker's intended meaning opposes their literal words. In Italian NLP, the standard benchmark is \textit{IronITA} \cite{ironita2018}, curated for EVALITA 2018 with nearly 5,000 tweets annotated for irony and sarcasm (defined as hostile or denigratory mockery). Results on IronITA showed that text-only classifiers struggle on short posts when pragmatic context is missing. To provide our models with broader training coverage on figurative language, we concatenate the external IronITA dataset to the MultiEMiT training split when training our UmBERTo baseline, while evaluating strictly on MultiEMiT test splits.

Linguistic text only tells half the story in modern social media. In their study on multimodal document intent, Kruk et al. \cite{MultiM} demonstrated that images in social posts rarely just illustrate captions; rather, they alter communicative intent through three distinct semiotic relationships: \textit{parallel}, where image and text independently convey the same underlying meaning; \textit{additive}, where the two modalities amplify or modify one another; and \textit{divergent}, where visual and textual cues pull in opposite directions to create a semantic gap. Incongruent or divergent pairings are particularly critical in social media, as they frequently generate memes, irony, and satirical humor by subverting the literal text. However, Kruk et al. focused exclusively on English Instagram posts, leaving the interplay between text and visual media in Italian television commentary largely unexplored. \textit{MultiEMiT} addresses this gap by applying this semiotic framework directly to Italian Social TV.
