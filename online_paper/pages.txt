MultiEMiT: Multimodal Emotion, Irony, and
Semiotic Relation Detection in Italian Social TV
via Correlation Prompting
Andrea Author, Graduate Student Member, IEEE, Co-Author One, Member, IEEE, and Co-Author Two, Senior
Member, IEEE

Abstract—During live television broadcasts, viewers react on
social media with a blend of text, screengrabs, and memes.
Decoding affective intent in this stream is hard: posts are
short, labels are heavily skewed, and images often twist or
invert the written message. We present MultiEMiT, a multimodal
dataset of Italian Twitter commentary annotated across five
pragmatic dimensions: fine-grained emotions under Plutchik’s
taxonomy, irony and sarcasm, hate speech, target orientation
(broadcast content versus program container), and semiotic text–
image relationships (additive, parallel, divergent). We evaluate task-specialized Dual-Encoders (Italian BERT and Vision
Transformer with Classifier Chains) against an open-weights
generative model, Pixtral 12B. To exploit label dependencies,
we introduce correlation prompting: directly injecting empirical
cross-task priors into the input text. Visual context improves
multi-label emotion recognition, lifting Macro F1 by +13% over
text-only models. In irony detection, raw visual features introduce
semantic noise and degrade performance through the modality
gap; injecting correlation priors resolves this misalignment and
pushes Dual-Encoder accuracy to F1 = 0.740. Across all tasks,
lightweight discriminative models outperform the fine-tuned 12billion-parameter foundation model. Pixtral suffers from exposure bias and repetitive token loops on long-tail categories,
requiring post-hoc truncation (n = 2) to eliminate hallucinations.
Index Terms—Affective computing, emotion detection, irony
detection, multimodal fusion, Social TV, correlation prompting,
vision-language models, Italian NLP.

silence. During live debates, political talk shows, and
reality competitions, viewers actively turn to second-screen
platforms like Twitter to share commentary, memes, and
screenshots [2]. This stream of real-time viewer feedback
offers broadcasters far richer signals than traditional audience
ratings (such as Auditel), providing immediate insight into

Capturing these affective signals computationally is difficult. Social TV commentary is short, colloquial, and inherently

While multilingual initiatives have advanced text-based
emotion analysis, computational resources for Italian remain
scarce. Existing Italian benchmarks either focus exclusively on
unimodal text [3], [9] or examine general web comments rather
than televised social discourse [8]. At the same time, pairing
text and images in satirical or reality programming introduces
heavy class imbalance and frequent semantic dissonance,
confounding standard vision-language models.
To study these dynamics, we introduce MultiEMiT, a
multimodal dataset of Italian Twitter commentary gathered
from television broadcasts. MultiEMiT provides annotations
across five pragmatic layers: fine-grained emotions under
Plutchik’s taxonomy [4], irony and sarcasm, hate speech, target
orientation (program container versus broadcast content), and
semiotic text–image relationships [5]. Our benchmarking focuses on three primary classification tasks: Emotion Detection,
Irony Detection, and Semiotic Relation Detection. The remaining annotations (hate speech and target) serve as contextual
signals. To expand training coverage for baseline text encoders,
we also draw on the larger unimodal EMiT corpus [3].
Our exploratory analysis uncovered strong empirical correlations between annotation layers: irony correlates with joy,
sarcasm pairs with disgust and hostility, and trust associates
with program loyalty. Rather than leaving neural models to
discover these sparse associations on their own, we propose
Correlation Prompting. This approach converts empirical
Pearson correlation statistics directly into textual prompt prefixes, providing explicit inductive priors during training and
inference.
To evaluate these methods, we frame our study around three
core research questions:
• RQ1 (Visual Context): Does adding visual context help
models detect emotion and irony, or do images introduce
distracting noise in non-literal posts?
• RQ2 (Correlation Prompting): Does injecting statistical
label correlations directly into text inputs improve accuracy across discriminative and generative architectures?
• RQ3 (Architectural Paradigms): How do compact,
task-specialized discriminative models (BERT–ViT DualEncoders with Classifier Chains) compare with a 12billion-parameter foundation model (Pixtral 12B)?
The rest of this paper explores these questions systematically. Section II situates MultiEMiT within Italian affective
computing, Social TV analytics, and multimodal document
intent. Section III describes the corpus collection, annotation

II. R ELATED W ORKS
Italian Affective and Emotion Corpora: Most annotated
resources for affective computing focus on English. While
multilingual initiatives like BRIGHTER [18] aggregate emotion data across 28 languages and 100,000 instances, they
omit Italian, pointing users toward standalone external sets
like FEEL-IT [9]. This gap leaves Italian social media underrepresented in multi-label benchmarks, despite distinct cultural
and linguistic norms in regional political discourse.
Early Italian resources focused strictly on binary or threeclass polarity. The first dedicated shift toward fine-grained
categories came with FEEL-IT [9], which collected 2,037
tweets across politics, sports, and healthcare during 2020. Annotated with Ekman’s four basic emotions (anger, joy, sadness,
fear), FEEL-IT established that Italian language models like
UmBERTo [12] produce reliable affective classifiers. Yet a
four-class schema misses mixed feelings and conversational
nuances common in viewer reactions. To address broader
emotional ranges, MultiEmotions-It [8] annotated YouTube
and Facebook comments across Plutchik’s eight primary emotions [4], polarity, and sarcasm. Their findings showed that
while polarity models perform consistently, multi-label emotion recognition and sarcasm detection suffer from severe class
sparsity and label ambiguity.
Social TV and Audience Monitoring (M.EMO.RAI and
EMiT): Second-screen platforms have changed how broadcasters analyze viewer engagement [2]. Traditional television ratings (like Auditel) measure raw viewer counts; they
cannot gauge whether an audience is laughing, outraged, or
sympathetic. The M.EMO.RAI project was formed to address
this limitation, establishing a joint partnership between the
University of Turin and Rai (Radiotelevisione Italiana) [10].
The goal was an automated system to track audience reactions
during live broadcasts by ingesting public social network
commentary.
This partnership led to the EMiT (Emotions in Italian)
corpus, benchmarked as an EVALITA 2023 shared task [3].
With 5,966 social media posts from live broadcasts (including
the Sanremo Festival and scripted drama), EMiT adopted
Plutchik’s taxonomy. It also introduced the Target dimension, distinguishing whether a comment attacks the broadcast
Content (the topic, guest, or political issue) or evaluates the
Container (the host, studio format, or network production).
EVALITA benchmark results showed that while classifiers
perform well on familiar shows, accuracy drops on out-ofdomain broadcasts. We use EMiT as an external textual corpus
to pre-train and enrich our baseline emotion encoders.
Irony Detection and Multimodal Intent: Irony detection
requires models to recognize when a speaker’s intended meaning opposes their literal words. In Italian NLP, the standard

2

benchmark is IronITA [11], curated for EVALITA 2018 with
nearly 5,000 tweets annotated for irony and sarcasm (defined
as hostile or denigratory mockery). Results on IronITA showed
that text-only classifiers struggle on short posts when pragmatic context is missing. To provide our models with domainadapted pragmatic competence, we use IronITA to fine-tune
UmBERTo before evaluating on television commentary.
Linguistic text only tells half the story in modern social
media. In their study on document intent, Kruk et al. [5]
demonstrated that images in social posts rarely just illustrate
captions; they alter communicative intent through distinct
semiotic relationships, ranging from complementary addition
to direct divergence. Incongruent or divergent pairings generate satirical humor and memes, where the image subverts
the text. Kruk et al. studied English Instagram posts. The
semiotic relationship between text and visual media in Italian
political satire and reality television remains largely unstudied.
MultiEMiT applies this semiotic framework directly to Italian
Social TV.
III. DATASET A NALYSIS
We introduce the MultiEMiT corpus, a multi-layered multimodal dataset of Italian Twitter commentary gathered during live television broadcasts. The data originates from the
M.EMO.RAI project [10], which set out to track audience
emotional responses in real time. Rather than restricting annotations to positive or negative sentiment, MultiEMiT applies
an expanded eight-emotion taxonomy derived from Plutchik’s
psychoevolutionary model [4].
Data Sources and Broadcast Registers: MultiEMiT draws
from two television broadcasts representing contrasting formats and registers:
ProSAT: Twitter commentary on Propaganda Live, a satirical
talk show on LA7 combining investigative journalism and
political commentary [6]. The collection captures the broadcast
of November 19, 2021, when host Diego Bianchi reported live
from the Poland–Belarus border during a humanitarian crisis.
The gravity of the event prompted solemn reactions dominated
by indignation, grief, and civic debate.
EMiT (Pechino Express): Collected as an extension of the
EVALITA EMiT benchmark [3], this sub-corpus covers
Pechino Express, an adventure reality show broadcast on Rai2
[7]. Collected during the season premiere on February 11,
2020, viewer discourse centers on lighthearted entertainment,
contestant rivalries, and humorous commentary.
Both partitions share an identical structure: a purely textual
partition and a multimodal partition pairing the text with its
attached media (screenshots, edited images, and memes). This
paired structure allows direct comparison between unimodal
and multimodal models, showing where visual content modulates the written post. In addition to MultiEMiT, we use the
external textual EMiT corpus (5,966 posts across multiple
programs [3]) as supplementary training data for baseline
emotion encoders.
Annotation Schema and Quality: Annotation was conducted
on Sensitag, a web annotation platform developed at the
University of Turin. Each post was evaluated independently

IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON AFFECTIVE COMPUTING, VOL. XX, NO. X, OCTOBER 2026

by three annotators with backgrounds in computer science or
linguistics. Final labels were assigned by majority vote (at
least two out of three annotators agreeing).
The corpus is annotated across five pragmatic dimensions:
• Emotions: Ten affective categories based on Plutchik’s
model [4] (Joy–Sadness, Trust–Disgust, Fear–Anger, Surprise–Anticipation), supplemented with Love and Neutral.
This task is multi-label, allowing posts to carry multiple
concurrent emotional states.
• Irony and Sarcasm: Three exclusive classes separating
literal statements (Unironic), humorous figurative inversion (Irony), and hostile, denigratory mockery (Sarcasm).
• Hate Speech: Identifies abusive language, insults, or
harassment directed at individuals or protected groups.
• Target: Distinguishes whether comments address the
broadcast Content (political issues, guests, or news
events) or the Container (the studio host, network, or
production execution).
• Semiotic Relation: Categorizes multimodal document
intent [5] into Additive (modality enrichment), Parallel
(redundant information), and Divergent (ironic contrast
or meme dissonance).
Semantic Ambiguity and Annotator Agreement: Sarcasm
and emotion in short social media posts are inherently ambiguous. Read in isolation, a comment on the border crisis
such as “Terrible, I am ashamed to be European” could be
seen as shame (Disgust + Fear), contempt (Anger + Disgust),
or disapproval (Surprise + Sadness).
Providing annotators with the attached image resolved much
of this ambiguity, substantially lifting inter-annotator agreement (Fleiss’ κ) across most emotion labels. Seeing photos of
shivering refugees grounded the context, directing consensus
toward sadness and distress. This empirical finding provides
direct justification for multimodal modeling: if visual context
clarifies ambiguous intent for human annotators, multimodal
neural architectures should benefit from the same disambiguating cues.
Dataset Distribution: Following gold-standard consolidation,
the MultiEMiT corpus resulted in two main partitions: a purely
Textual dataset comprising 2,909 posts, and a Multimodal
dataset comprising 645 posts with paired image attachments.
As typical of social media discourse, label distributions
exhibit pronounced class imbalance. Tables I and II present the
complete distribution of labels across both partitions, broken
down by broadcast sub-corpus.
Across both partitions, the most frequent affective states are
Trust and Joy, indicating an overall positive engagement with
broadcast programming. However, negative emotions exhibit
marked domain dependency: Sadness, Anger, and Fear are
heavily concentrated within ProSAT due to the geopolitical
reportage, whereas they remain negligible in the lighthearted
Pechino Express subset.
Similarly, irony displays genre-specific dynamics. In the
multimodal corpus, irony is prevalent in Pechino Express (88
instances) despite Propaganda Live being a satirical format.
This occurs because the specific Propaganda Live episode
under study addressed an acute humanitarian crisis, prompting solemn reflection rather than playful sarcasm. Regarding

strong mutual predictors.

IV. E XPERIMENTAL D ESIGN
This section details the experimental methodology established to evaluate affective classification on the MultiEMiT
corpus. We evaluate three distinct tasks: Emotion Detection,
Irony Detection, and Semiotic Relation Detection. The experiments measure the performance differences between unimodal
textual baselines, a discriminative multimodal architecture
(Dual-Encoder), and a generative foundation model (Pixtral

12B), while assessing the empirical impact of correlation
prompting across architectures.
Task Definition and Evaluation Metrics: We formalize three
classification benchmarks corresponding to different levels of
communicative intent:
• Emotion Detection: Multi-label classification over ten
candidate emotions (Anger, Anticipation, Disgust, Fear,
Joy, Love, Neutral, Sadness, Surprise, and Trust). Models
predict any valid subset of concurrent emotions using
binary cross-entropy or binary chains.
• Irony Detection: Single-label classification categorizing
each post into one of three mutually exclusive classes:
Unironic, Irony, or Sarcasm.
• Semiotic Relation Detection: Single-label classification
identifying the semantic text–image interaction as Additive, Parallel, or Divergent.
Due to heavy class skew, raw accuracy paints a misleading
picture of model quality. We therefore evaluate systems using
Precision, Recall, and the F1-score. In overall comparisons
across all three tasks, the Macro F1-score serves as the
primary metric, weighting each class equally to penalize
poor performance on minority labels. In fine-grained subset
evaluations across semiotic relations (Section V), we report
the Micro F1-score to maintain metric stability when sample
sizes in individual subsets are small.
Unimodal Textual Baselines: To establish strong text-only
reference points, we evaluated models built on the RoBERTa
architecture. For Irony and Semiotic Relation detection, we
used UmBERTo [12], a RoBERTa-based language model pretrained on Italian Common Crawl with whole-word masking.
For Irony detection, UmBERTo was first fine-tuned on the
IronITA benchmark [11] before adaptation to MultiEMiT. For
Emotion Detection, we used FEEL-IT [9], fine-tuned on the
combined MultiEMiT textual split and the external EMiT

Multimodal Dual-Encoder Architecture: To capture crossmodal interactions, we implemented a discriminative DualEncoder pipeline (Fig. 2). The textual modality is processed
by the domain-adapted language encoder (FEEL-IT for emotions, UmBERTo for irony and relations), while the visual
modality is processed in parallel by a Vision Transformer
(ViT-base-patch16-224) [14]. Both unimodal encoders extract
high-level representations: the 768-dimensional ‘[CLS]‘ token
embedding from the text transformer and the 768-dimensional
pooled representation from the ViT are concatenated into a
1536-dimensional joint multimodal vector.
To predict class labels from this joint representation, we
employ Classifier Chains [16]. This architecture models label interdependencies by sequentially conditioning subsequent binary classifiers on previous predictions, making it
well suited for multi-label emotion spaces. Specifically, we
configure Scikit-Learn’s ClassifierChain wrapping an
SGDClassifier with logistic loss, L2 regularization, and
fixed random seeding. This linear head projects the concatenated latent space to class predictions without overfitting on
the compact multimodal split.
Correlation Prompting Framework: Motivated by the strong
label dependencies discovered during corpus analysis (Section III), we developed a structured Correlation Prompting
strategy. Rather than hoping the neural network will infer
subtle cross-task correlations from scarce training examples,
we inject empirical Pearson correlation coefficients directly
into the input text as natural language context. For example,
in the Irony detection task, the input tweet is appended with:
“Statistics: Sarcasm correlates positively with Disgust and
Anger. Irony correlates positively with Divergent relation.”
To evaluate model sensitivity to the volume of injected
priors, we structured prompts across three distinct correlation tiers based on Pearson coefficient thresholds: (i) Short
prompts, incorporating only strong associations with |r| ≥
0.25; (ii) Medium prompts, including moderate correlations
with |r| ≥ 0.20; and (iii) Long prompts, providing detailed

5

contextual priors with all correlations satisfying |r| ≥ 0.15.
All prompt instructions were formulated in English, following
findings from the BRIGHTER benchmark [18] indicating that
multilingual LLMs adhere better to instructions and exhibit
lower variance when prompted in English rather than the target
language.
Large Multimodal Model (Pixtral 12B): To compare specialized discriminative systems against modern generative visionlanguage models, we benchmarked Pixtral 12B [17], an openweights multimodal language model based on Mistral Nemo
featuring a dedicated 400M-parameter vision encoder capable
of handling arbitrary image resolutions. We evaluated Pixtral
across three experimental regimes:
• Zero-Shot Prompting: Out-of-the-box reasoning across
prompt tiers (Short, Medium, and three Long prompt formulations: long v1, long v2, long v3), testing generation
with and without correlation context.
• Few-Shot (Three-Shot) Prompting: In-context learning
for Semiotic Relation Detection, providing three complete
multimodal exemplars (one per relation category) within
the prompt window.

Generative Hallucinations and the Truncation Parameter
(n): Unlike discriminative encoders that output bounded probability vectors over predefined classes, Pixtral generates labels
autoregressively. During multi-label emotion fine-tuning, the
model exhibited a systematic failure mode: when prompted to
produce a list of emotions, Pixtral frequently entered repetitive
token loops or produced out-of-vocabulary synonyms outside
the ten-class taxonomy (such as generating Joy, Joy, Happiness
instead of Joy). This behavior stems from ranking bias and
exposure bias leading to the degradation of the stopping token
(<EOS>).
To correct this without retraining, we implemented a postprocessing truncation parameter n, restricting extraction to the
first n valid vocabulary emotions generated. For multi-label
emotion detection, setting n = 2 proved optimal, filtering out
unconstrained tail hallucinations and matching the empirical
distribution of the gold standard, where the vast majority of
posts express one or two concurrent emotions.

Detection. We analyze the quantitative impact of integrating visual features (comparing blind text baselines to the
Dual-Encoder), the performance gains induced by correlation
prompting, and the strengths and failure modes of Pixtral

Emotion Detection: Emotion detection represents the most
challenging benchmark due to the high cardinality of the
ten-class label space and the multi-label nature of affective
expression in social media.
On the textual dataset, the baseline discriminative model
(FEEL-IT fine-tuned on MultiEMiT and EMiT) achieved a
Macro F1 -score of 0.460, increasing to 0.540 when trained
with correlation prompting, and reaching 0.590 when retrained
across two isolated phases with correlation context. When
evaluating exclusively on the multimodal test set, the blind
text-only baseline (evaluating only the text of multimodal
posts) achieves a Macro F1 -score of 0.300 without correlations
and 0.370 with correlations.
Introducing visual features via the Dual-Encoder architecture substantially enhances classification performance: the
multimodal system achieves a Macro F1 -score of 0.430 without correlations and 0.450 with correlations. This marked
improvement (+13% absolute over the blind unprompted text
baseline) empirically confirms that visual context provides
essential disambiguating cues for recognizing emotional expressions in social commentary, with correlation prompting
providing a complementary gain.
Turning to generative foundation models, fine-tuned Pixtral
12B achieved an F1 -score of 0.426 (T = 0.1). While competitive with the discriminative Dual-Encoder without correlations (0.430), it remains below the correlation-prompted DualEncoder (0.450), showing that task-adapted discriminative
representations remain superior for multi-label Italian emotion
classification. Complete comparisons across prompt tiers and

VI. C ONCLUSION
We presented MultiEMiT, a multimodal dataset of Italian
Twitter commentary annotated across five pragmatic layers:
Plutchik emotions, irony, hate speech, target, and semiotic
relations. Our benchmark compared specialized discriminative
Dual-Encoders against Pixtral 12B across three core research
questions:
• RQ1 (Visual Context): Adding images helps when text
and image agree, boosting multi-label emotion recognition by +13% Macro F1 over text baselines. In irony,
however, unguided visual features act as semantic noise,
causing the unprompted multimodal model to drop below
the text baseline due to the modality gap.
• RQ2 (Correlation Prompting): Injecting empirical correlation statistics as text prefixes improves accuracy
across tasks. For irony, correlation prompting anchors
the multimodal representation, lifting the Dual-Encoder
to peak performance (F1 = 0.740).
• RQ3 (Architectural Paradigms): Compact discriminative models (BERT–ViT with Classifier Chains) consistently beat the 12B-parameter foundation model on
domain-specific Italian commentary. Pixtral suffers from
exposure bias and unconstrained repetition on long-tail
classes, requiring truncation (n = 2) to eliminate hallucinations.
Looking ahead, we are integrating the Dual-Encoder framework into Rai’s live M.EMO.RAI monitoring pipeline for realtime broadcast analysis. We also plan to expand MultiEMiT to
live sports and cultural events, testing custom labels for viewer
engagement such as suspense and schadenfreude.
